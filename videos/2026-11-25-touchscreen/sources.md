# Sources: How a touchscreen knows where your finger is (episode 8, long, for 19:00 on 25 Nov 2026)

| Claim in the video | Source(s) |
| --- | --- |
| A phone's (capacitive) touchscreen doesn't need pressure | ScienceAlert: https://www.sciencealert.com/how-does-a-touchscreen-actually-work ("capacitive touchscreens don't respond to pressure at all"); Electronic Design: https://www.electronicdesign.com/displays/what-s-difference-between-resistive-and-capacitive-touchscreens ("Projected capacitance does not use pressure for touch detection—it can detect even the lightest of touches") |
| Skin is a conductor, so your finger pulls a little charge from the screen where you touch | MicroTouch: https://microtouch.com/capacitive-technology/ ("When your finger touches the surface of the cover glass it pulls a small charge from the electrodes"); ScienceAlert ("Human skin conducts electricity", "your finger decreases the charge at the intersection of the grid"); Popular Science: https://www.popsci.com/why-does-drop-water-confuse-my-touchscreen/ (a finger "sucks out some of the charge") |
| A plastic pen or an ordinary glove doesn't work (insulators) | ScienceAlert ("you can't operate a smartphone with gloves or a plastic stylus, which are both essentially insulators"); Explain That Stuff: https://www.explainthatstuff.com/touchscreens.html (plastic stylus "is an insulator"); Nelson Miller: https://nelson-miller.com/why-cant-i-wear-gloves-with-my-touchscreen (gloves "restrict the flows of electricity") |
| Under the glass is a grid of rows and columns of transparent wires made of indium tin oxide | MicroTouch ("electrodes that are configured into rows and columns", "made of a transparent conductive coating called indium tin oxide (ITO)"); ScienceAlert ("an array of horizontal and vertical transparent conductive wires", "typically made out of indium tin oxide"); arXiv overview: https://arxiv.org/pdf/1612.08227 ("a matrix of rows and columns of conductive electrodes", attached to the display glass) |
| A chip measures the charge at every crossing, continually, and works out the exact spot | MicroTouch ("The controller continually scans and monitors the capacitance of the electrodes", "the controller can determine the exact location of your touch"); arXiv 1612.08227 ("The capacitance can be changed and measured at every individual point on the grid", "able to accurately estimate the touch position") |
| Measuring each crossing lets it follow several fingers at once; pinch to zoom is a two-finger multi-touch gesture | arXiv 1612.08227 (mutual capacitive sensing "provides the capability of detecting multi-touch positions"); MicroTouch ("multi-touch capability"); Electronic Design ("Projected capacitance can also support multi-finger touch input and gestures"); Computer Hope: https://computerhope.com/jargon/p/pinch-to-zoom.htm (pinch to zoom: "touch two fingers ... on the surface" of a screen "that supports multi-touch") |
| Water is a conductor too, so rain or drops on the screen can register as touches | AIP Inside Science: https://www.aip.org/inside-science/why-does-water-confuse-my-touch-screen ("water may also conduct electricity just like your finger"; screens "may view ... a drop of water, the same way it does a finger touch"); Popular Science ("Water conducts electricity, just like your finger"; "drops of sweat or rain can reduce the charge too") |
| In 2010, cold commuters in South Korea were reported using sausages as a stylus, because electrically they're close enough to a finger (said as "were reported") | Engadget, 12 Feb 2010: https://www.engadget.com/2010-02-12-frozen-sausage-as-iphone-stylus.html (CJ Corporation saw sausage sales rise "because cold commuters have discovered that they make an effective" stylus); The Next Web, 11 Feb 2010: https://thenextweb.com/news/cold-frozen-sausage-sales-roof (sausages used as a "touch screen smartphone stylus" in South Korea; "electrostatically speaking", they are close approximations of the human finger); Engadget also: "electrostatically compatible ... nearly as effective as using a human finger" |
| (Comment question only) a nose works on the screen | ScienceAlert ("swiping the screen with your nose will work just as well as a finger") |

Dropped: a scan-rate figure (no phone-specific Hz source found) and the 1965 E.A. Johnson invention story (not checked against two sources today).
