# Sources: How a key opens a lock (#10, 27 Nov 2026)

Each claim has one primary source or two independent reputable ones. Quotes are the pages' own wording.

| Claim in the video | Sources |
| --- | --- |
| Inside the lock are stacks of pins split in two (a bottom "key pin" and a top "driver pin"), pushed down by springs | [Wikipedia: Pin tumbler lock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pin_tumbler_lock) ("Above each key pin is one or more spring-loaded driver pins"; "the pins are pushed down into the plug by the springs"); [HowStuffWorks](https://home.howstuffworks.com/home-improvement/household-safety/lock-picking4.htm) ("The shafts of a pin-and-tumbler lock contain several springs and tiny pins") |
| A typical lock has five or six pin stacks | [Wikipedia: Pin tumbler lock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pin_tumbler_lock) ("typically five or six of them"); [Britannica: Lock](https://www.britannica.com/technology/lock-security) ("the pins (usually five)") |
| With no key, the top pins sit across the gap between the plug (the part that turns) and the case, so it can't turn | [Wikipedia: Pin tumbler lock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pin_tumbler_lock) ("The point where the plug and cylinder meet is called the shear point"; "When the key is not in the lock, the pins straddle the shear point, preventing the plug from rotating") |
| The right key lifts each stack so its split lands exactly on that gap, and the plug turns | [Wikipedia: Pin tumbler lock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pin_tumbler_lock) ("the pins will rise causing them to align exactly at the shear point … This allows the plug to rotate"); [HowStuffWorks](https://home.howstuffworks.com/home-improvement/household-safety/lock-picking4.htm) ("there is a gap at the shear line and the key is able to turn"); [Wikipedia: Bitting (key)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bitting_(key)) ("lift each tumbler to a predetermined height to the shear line") |
| A wrong key leaves the pins misaligned, so a pin still blocks the gap | [Wikipedia: Pin tumbler lock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pin_tumbler_lock) ("If the wrong key is used, the pins remain misaligned, preventing the plug from turning"); follows from the shear-line sources above |
| A key can be written down as a number, one digit per cut, giving its depth | [Wikipedia: Bitting (key)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bitting_(key)) ("usually a series of integers (e.g. 372164) … each digit … corresponds to a certain location on the key blank … and also indicates the necessary depth of the cut"); [Master Lock technical manual, locksmith glossary (ALOA/LIST Council terms)](https://cdn.masterlock.com/masterlock/resources/documents/pdf/7000-0031_Technical_Manual_pg_30_38.pdf) ("bitting … the number(s) which represent(s) the dimensions of the key"; "bitting depth … the depth of a cut"); [CLK Supplies](https://clksupplies.com/blogs/news/lock-code-series-must-know-info) ("the key bitting … are 2, 4, 2, 4, 5, 4, 5, 5 … the cuts for the key") |
| Master-keyed locks have an extra small piece in some stacks, so a stack can split at two heights and two different keys both open it; that's how one master key opens many locks that each have their own key | [Wikipedia: Master keying](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Master_keying) ("two shear points at each pin position, one for the change key and one for the master key"); [HowStuffWorks: How does a master key work?](https://home.howstuffworks.com/home-improvement/household-safety/question710.htm) ("This third pin is called a master wafer or spacer"; "ideal for apartment buildings, office complexes and hotels"); [Wikipedia: Lock and key](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lock_and_key) ("An additional pin called the master pin is present between the key and driver pins in locks that accept master keys") |
| Ancient Egyptians used wooden pin locks on the same idea about 4,000 years ago | [Britannica: Lock](https://www.britannica.com/technology/lock-security) ("Possibly 4,000 years old, it is of the type known as a pin tumbler or, from its widespread use in Egypt, an Egyptian lock"); [ASME: Linus Yale Jr.](https://www.asme.org/topics-resources/content/linus-yale-jr) ("the large wooden locks the ancient Egyptians built some 4,000 years ago"); [Lemelson-MIT: Linus Yale](https://lemelson.mit.edu/resources/linus-yale) ("used in ancient Egypt around 4,000 years ago"); [Wikipedia: Lock and key](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lock_and_key) (the modern lock is "essentially a more developed version of the Egyptian lock") |
| In the 1860s Linus Yale Jr. gave it the small, flat key with a jagged (serrated) edge that's still used today | [Britannica: Lock](https://www.britannica.com/technology/lock-security) ("In the 1860s his son Linus Yale, Jr., evolved the Yale cylinder lock, with its small, flat key with serrated edge"); [Lemelson-MIT](https://lemelson.mit.edu/resources/linus-yale) ("Yale patented his cylinder pin-tumbler lock in 1861"); [ASME](https://www.asme.org/topics-resources/content/linus-yale-jr) ("small flat key (patented 1861), which is still used today"); [Wikipedia: Lock and key](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lock_and_key) ("still remains in use today") |

## Dropped or softened
- "Invented in Egypt": Wikipedia puts the earliest known examples in Mesopotamia/Assyria, so the video says Egyptians *used* them.
- Linus Yale Sr.'s exact year: 1848 (Wikipedia, Britannica) vs 1843 (Gizmodo), so he's left out.
- Yale Jr.'s exact year: 1861 (Lemelson, Wikipedia) vs 1865 (ASME body text), so the video says "the 1860s" (Britannica).
- "Too high or too low": no source says it in those words for an ordinary wrong key; the video says a pin "still blocks the gap".
- Number of possible cut depths (e.g. ten): no source found, left out.
- No lock-picking or bypass content, by design.
